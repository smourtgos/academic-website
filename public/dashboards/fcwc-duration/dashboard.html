Interrogation Duration and the Estimation of FCWC Risk

Does a six-hour interrogation carry a 10% risk of producing a false confession wrongful conviction? Move the inputs and watch the answer move with them.

Based on Mourtgos & Adams, a reply to Smith et al. (2026), forthcoming in the Journal of Criminal Justice

The short version. Smith et al. (2026) replicated our earlier analysis of false confession wrongful conviction (FCWC) risk — and their replication confirms its central point. They then asked a further question: does FCWC risk rise with interrogation duration? Their model reports roughly 10% risk at six hours and 25% at twelve.

We examined whether the available evidence supports those numbers. It does not. The two duration distributions the model compares are built from different populations, different outcomes, different units of observation, and different measures of duration. Change which false confession study supplies one of them — changing nothing else — and the twelve-hour estimate moves from 26.3% to 72.6%.

What this does not show. Nothing here establishes that prolonged interrogations are safe, or that duration is unrelated to false confession. The finding is narrower and about measurement: the reported thresholds are not stable estimates of population risk.

Neither Source Estimates What the Model Needs

Drizin & Leo (2004)

Duration is reported for only 44 of 125 false confession cases. The authors note in a footnote that some reported durations may reflect time in custody rather than active questioning, and that some newspaper accounts could not be independently verified. Only 44 cases ended in conviction, and the published table does not report their durations separately.

Redlich et al. (2011)

Cases are self-reported false confessions rather than verified wrongful convictions; 17% reportedly ended in dismissal. The authors caution that retrospective time estimates are vulnerable to memory error, that mental illness may compound this, and that their findings may not generalize to offenders without mental health problems.

The fitted Redlich distribution places about 12.1% of its mass at six hours or more and 3.2% at twelve hours or more. In Drizin and Leo's duration table the corresponding proportions are 84% and at least 50%. Treating either source as the duration distribution of FCWCs requires assuming it can stand in for false confessions that produced wrongful convictions.

Six hours is not a fixed feature of the data. The reported thresholds sit far out in the tails of fitted curves, where almost nothing was actually observed. Under the published specification, only about 0.116% of the fitted comparison distribution lies above 12.06 hours, and 0.0024% above 34 hours. Those are outputs of an assumed curve, not observed frequencies.

So the estimate depends heavily on how fast the comparison curve is assumed to fall toward zero, and on which studies are allowed into it. Change either below.

Applying Smith et al.'s own bootstrap procedure to both duration likelihoods and carrying the draws through Bayes' theorem, with the base rate fixed at .019, gives these 95% intervals. At the point where the published estimate reaches 10%, the uncertainty in the fitted likelihoods places the risk on either side of that threshold.

The bootstrap still assumes the selected studies, measurements, weights and lognormal family are appropriate. It quantifies uncertainty inside the model; it cannot resolve the comparability problems that motivated this reply.

What the comparison group is made of. The non-FCWC likelihood pools twelve sets of summary statistics from eight studies, weighted by sample size. But those sample sizes count different things — and the weighting does not know the difference.

Two Studies, Two Units
What one unit of n means in each.

Kassin et al.'s (2007) n = 601 counts officers estimating their typical interrogation length. Kelly et al.'s (2016) n = 29 counts 29 recorded interrogations.

Weighting by sample size gives the officer survey about 21 times the influence of the recorded study, although the two count and measure different things.

The same issue affects the reported variation. Kassin et al.'s standard deviation describes variation across officers' estimates of a typical interrogation, not variation across individual interrogations. The model nonetheless uses it to determine how often individual interrogations run into the right tail.

A Comparison Within One Study

Redlich et al. (2011) allow a more comparable, though still limited, check: both confession groups came from the same population and answered the same questions. Keeping the false confession curve and replacing the pooled comparison group with Redlich's own 30 true confessors gives a very different picture.

This is not a corrected population estimate. Redlich et al.'s sample is small, retrospective, and restricted to persons with serious mental illness. What it shows is narrower: the sharp increase in the published model does not come from the difference observed between the two groups within that study. It appears when that study's false confessors are compared against the narrower pooled distribution assembled from other sources.
Total duration is partly an outcome, not just exposure ▼

Smith et al. justify conditioning on duration by arguing that longer exposure necessarily raises the cumulative probability of a false confession. That argument requires duration to measure exposure preceding the confession. The measure used does not.

Suppose a suspect confesses after two hours and is questioned for four more. The model codes the confession as occurring in a six-hour interrogation, although only two hours preceded it. Part of the measured exposure therefore occurs after the outcome it is meant to explain.

The model can estimate an association between FCWC and completed duration. It cannot show how the probability of an initial false confession changes with each additional hour of questioning.

Custodial delay and active questioning are different measures ▼

Cleary and Bull's (2021) field study of 249 jail detainees measured the time between detention and interrogation separately from the duration of the interrogation itself. Neither measure was significantly associated with whether respondents reported a full confession, a partial admission, or a denial.

Because the researchers did not ask participants whether they were guilty, these findings cannot establish a relationship between duration and false confession. They do demonstrate that custodial delay and active questioning are distinct measures, and that neither was significantly related to confession outcome in that sample.

What the within-study duration test actually found ▼

Redlich et al.'s duration measure was cumulative questioning across multiple sittings. Total questioning averaged 3.07 hours among the 35 false confessors and 1.94 hours among the 30 true confessors — a difference that was not statistically significant (F(1, 63) = 1.45, p = .23, d = 0.30).

A nonsignificant result in a sample this small does not establish equivalence. But it does mean the study itself does not establish a pronounced difference in total duration. When the authors examined the longest individual questioning period, roughly 6–7% of both groups reported a session lasting six hours or more.

The underlying problem is a missing denominator. False confessions unquestionably occur, and prolonged interrogations may well increase their risk. Neither fact tells us the probability that an interrogation of a given length produces a false confession and a wrongful conviction.

Finding that prolonged questioning is common among known false confessors does not tell us whether a six-hour interrogation carries a 2%, 10%, or 50% risk — unless we also know how often six-hour interrogations occur without producing an FCWC.

What the Model Computes

P(FCWC | h)  =  π · f1(h)  ÷  [ π · f1(h)  +  (1 − π) · f0(h) ]

π is the FCWC base rate; f1 and f0 are fitted duration densities for FCWC and non-FCWC cases.

At a fixed base rate, the estimate depends on the ratio f1(h) / f0(h): how much more common a duration is under the fitted FCWC curve than under the fitted comparison curve. A large estimate can therefore appear simply because the comparison curve approaches zero — even when both curves rest on very few observations at that duration.

So the empirical meaning of the output depends entirely on whether the two curves represent comparable measurements from comparable populations, especially in their tails. Here they differ in outcome, population, unit of observation, and duration measure. The resulting likelihood ratio reflects those differences alongside any real association between duration and FCWC status.

What the Field Still Needs

The exchange leaves a concrete empirical task, and one on which both sets of authors agree: code actual interrogations for tactic presence, duration and outcome as recordings become more widely available. Recording alone, though, will not supply the denominator. Researchers would also need to:

RequirementWhy it is needed
Define the sampling populationPopulation risk cannot be estimated without knowing which interrogations could have entered the sample.
Separate sessions from cumulative questioningA 3.07-hour total across multiple sittings is not a 3.07-hour interrogation.
Record the time of first admissionOtherwise questioning that followed the confession is counted as exposure preceding it.
Link interrogation features to case outcomesDuration data without outcomes cannot identify risk.
Distinguish a false confession from one that produced a wrongful convictionThese are different events, and the model's target quantity is the second.
On the magnitude and the judgment. How large the risk is and whether it is acceptable are separate questions. Smith et al. conclude that even a 1% FCWC rate is problematic. We agree — as our original article put it, a society may judge even low probabilities unacceptable, and such judgments are ethical and political rather than empirical. The disagreement here concerns whether the available evidence establishes the magnitude. The seriousness of an outcome informs how much risk society should tolerate; it cannot establish how often that outcome occurs.
